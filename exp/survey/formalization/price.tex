\section{The price of the search}
\label{sec:price-search}

Section~\ref{sec:passive} names the two transfer hypotheses and states that
every floor in this framework comes from what the views withhold. This section
grounds both hypotheses in the sharpest current results and reports one
experiment. Everything here carries
its epistemic status inline, because the ground shifts: two of the units this
section could have been built on two years ago are now known to be
algorithm-relative artifacts.

\subsection{The price of aiming}
\label{sec:price-aiming}

The no-aiming hypothesis says that, below the known query counts, learning is
the only search over model space. Its in-model counterpart is a floor against a
view-generic adversary that must produce a model with a target property, and
the floor depends on the view of the data the game grants.

For recipes whose steps are statistical queries, the floor is unconditional. A
hidden $k$-sparse parity requires $d^{\Omega(k)}$ statistical queries at any
useful tolerance, and this is the number Barak et al.\ describe SGD as
approaching \cite{BEGKMZ22}. One hypothesis connects that floor to gradient
recipes, and its status must be stated. A fresh-batch gradient step of batch size
$B$ evaluates a correlational statistical query to tolerance on the order of
$B^{-1/2}$, up to concentration, so a $T$-step fresh-batch recipe reads as a
correlational-query algorithm with $T$ queries at that tolerance. The reading is
standard and is how Barak et al.\ compare SGD to the SQ limit
\cite{BEGKMZ22}, and it is not a theorem. Dandi et al.\ state the inheritance
for reused batches as an expectation explicitly \cite{DTAPZK24}. Every use of a
query floor for a gradient recipe in this framework routes through this
simulation and carries its status. For Gaussian single-index models the current sharp
single number is the generative exponent $k^*$. Any statistical-query algorithm
using polynomially many queries, under the coefficient condition its theorem
states, needs a sample-size parameter $n \gtrsim d^{k^*/2}$ to output a
direction with nontrivial overlap, unconditionally within the class
\cite{DPLB24}. Throughout this section $n$ is the sample count, as in the
sources, and the ambient security parameter does not appear. One caveat travels with
that citation and it runs in the safe direction: the authors state that their
theorem primarily measures the detection threshold, and the statistical-query
estimation threshold is open, so the true price of recovery can only be higher
than the floor quoted here. For multi-index models the same quantity governs
subspace recovery, with an unconditional estimation upper bound at the matching
rate and a low-degree lower bound \cite{DLB25}. Any reading of the low-degree
half as polynomial-time hardness must carry the refutation of the
quasi-polynomial low-degree conjecture and its surviving positive counterpart
\cite{BHJK25,HKKLMT26}.

Two units this section does not use, and why. The information exponent prices a
single algorithm, online SGD from a random start \cite{AGJ21}, and the leap's
lower bound holds for correlational statistical queries only, with its SGD side
an open conjecture \cite{ABM23}. Both thresholds collapse under batch reuse
\cite{DTAPZK24}, and the collapse is not an anomaly: reuse reaches the
information-theoretic rate for polynomial links \cite{LOSW24}, a learning-rate
schedule induces the same transition without carrying any sample across
iterations, though each sample is queried twice within its own iteration
\cite{TME25}, and full-batch descent separates from one-pass descent by a logarithmic
factor within the analyzed dynamics, with the separation proved for a truncated
variant of the target and provably absent for the plain one \cite{KJWSM26}. What survives this wave is the generative exponent, whose
defining invariance is exactly closure under the transformations reuse exploits
\cite{DPLB24}. Proven, all of it, in the classes named.

The algebraic escape calibrates the floor rather than refuting it. Noiseless
parity is solvable by Gaussian elimination from $O(d)$ raw samples. That
algorithm is generic for a game that grants the raw corpus and unavailable to
one whose view of the data is statistical queries, which is the view the floor
is proven against. Parity is therefore the case where the view classification
of Section~\ref{sec:class} is not hypothetical. The same task is easy under
one view and $d^{\Omega(k)}$-hard under another, and the gap between the two
views is a named theorem about what learning-shaped access cannot do.

\subsection{The price of skipping is recipe-relative}
\label{sec:price-skipping}

The no-skipping hypothesis as stated in Section~\ref{sec:passive} prices the
walk of one recipe. The literature now forces exactly that scoping, and the
force is worth exhibiting, because a stronger reading of the hypothesis is
false.

The walk's length is a property of the pair recipe and query type, not of the
task. The batch-reuse and learning-rate results above shorten the same walk to
the same target by changing the recipe slightly \cite{DTAPZK24,LOSW24,TME25},
and empirically the compute needed to reach a fixed language-model loss has
halved roughly every eight months, with a wide stated uncertainty of five to
fourteen months, across a decade of recipe drift \cite{HBEORGATS24}.
So a task-level no-skipping claim is refuted, and a recipe-level one is the
strongest defensible form. Within a fixed recipe, the compute-to-loss frontier
is a theorem in solvable models: for power-law random features under one-pass
stochastic gradient descent the loss curve is exact and the compute-optimal
frontier is a derived object \cite{PPXP24}, which is the theorem-shaped form of
the empirical scaling laws \cite{KMHBCCGRWA20,HBMBCRCHWCHNMDDGOSERVS22}, whose fitted constants
are themselves contested \cite{BEBY24}. The mechanism literature makes the
exponent explicitly algorithm-dependent: allowing feature learning nearly
doubles the compute exponent on hard tasks \cite{BAP25}, and a good data-pruning
rule changes the law's form outright \cite{SGSGM22}. None of these results claims
a lower bound over all algorithms, and no such bound exists. Open, and it is the
open problem under every compute-pricing claim in this survey.

Two consequences are definitional in the oracle model and consistent with the
empirical record. First, amortization is not skipping. Distillation reaches a
target cheaper only below a student-dependent compute threshold, and only when
a stronger teacher already exists or its training cost is amortized across
other uses, so the teacher's training was an oracle call somebody paid for
\cite{BSWRLW25}. In the model, a
party that builds a derivative model builds it from views of prior traces, so
the cost sits upstream in the ledger. There is no free trace, only amortized traces. Second, the
price of a view is not the price of the trace. Proof-of-learning verification
accepts a lossy view of the trace, and the published forgeries attack that view,
succeeding without paying the walk \cite{FJTYCDCP23}, while exact reproduction
of a trace remains empirically out of reach \cite{SHKBL25}. A sound
proof-of-learning is precisely a view whose price equals the trace's, and
whether one exists is the open problem that literature keeps encountering from
inside.

\subsection{An experiment: the measured exponent of the search}
\label{sec:price-experiment}

The transfer hypotheses assert that no cheaper route than the recipe exists,
and they predict nothing about how the recipe's own cost grows. The experiment
below therefore tests alignment, not the hypotheses. It measures how the walk
lengthens for one recipe on the problem whose query floor is unconditional, and
asks whether anything in the measurement contradicts that floor. Sparse parity is the cleanest instance, because the
statistical-query floor is unconditional and the statistical baseline of
$\Theta(k \log d)$ samples makes the gap between information and computation
visible. Barak et al.\ report the phase transition and its scaling
\cite{BEGKMZ22}. The run below matches their Figure-2 width, batch and learning rate, with one
stated difference, momentum 0.9 where they run SGD with no auxiliary state, over
a dimension range extending beyond their fitted grid of $n \le 30$, into the
range where their own plots show the exponent drifting, so that the survey's
numbers are its own.

A two-layer ReLU network of width 100 is trained by minibatch SGD with
momentum 0.9, batch 128, learning rate 0.01, on fresh samples per step, to
learn a hidden \ParityK-sparse parity of $d$ bits, for $d$ from \ParityDims{},
\ParitySeeds{} seeds per dimension. Test accuracy is checked every 25 steps, so
every recorded cost is a multiple of 25. The cost recorded is oracle steps to reach \ParityThresh{} test
accuracy. All \ParitySolved{} runs reached it. The median cost rises from
\ParityMedLoD{} steps at $d = 20$ to \ParityMedHiD{} at $d = 60$, and the
log-log fit across dimensions gives
\[
t_c \;\propto\; d^{\,\ParitySlope},
\]
Figure~\ref{fig:parity}. The fit spans five dimensions covering a factor of
three, the consecutive-point slopes of the medians range from 0.8 to 3.0, and
no goodness-of-fit statistic is claimed, so the fitted exponent summarizes a
short power-law stretch and is not a three-digit measurement. Three further
sentences fix what it is and is not. It sits in the power-law family $t_c \propto d^{\alpha k}$ that Barak et
al.\ document, whose exponent depends on the architecture, the batch and the
regime, and whose own scaling plots show it worsening at larger $d$
\cite{BEGKMZ22}. It is consistent with the query floor through the simulation
above, since the floor constrains the product of queries and squared tolerance
and fixes no step exponent at a given batch. It does not measure the floor, and
no experiment can, since a floor quantifies over algorithms. The convergence
times are heavy-tailed across seeds at fixed dimension, which matches the
initialization sensitivity Barak et al.\ document. A second regime changes three hyperparameters at once, batch 8, width 512 and
learning rate 0.1, so it isolates no single cause, and its wall is sharper than
any fit. The median over three seeds at $d = 30$ is \ProbeMedThirty{} steps,
and none of nine runs at $d \in \{60, 90, 120\}$ reached threshold within
\ProbeCap{} steps, a greater than forty-fold increase against a doubling of
dimension. Empirical, all of it, in the configurations stated.

What the experiment does and does not show. It shows one recipe's cost growing
as a power of the dimension, in the regime where the recipe's steps read as
noisy correlational queries. It does not measure the floor itself,
since no experiment can quantify over algorithms, and it says nothing about
adversaries granted richer views, for which noiseless parity is easy. It is one
recipe's cost curve, consistent with the no-aiming hypothesis for the class
that trains, and nothing more.
